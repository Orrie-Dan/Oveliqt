# How to Choose a Software Developer: 7 Questions to Ask Before You Hire

Hiring someone to build your website, app or business system is one of those decisions that's hard to judge from the outside. Everyone's portfolio looks good. Everyone promises quality. And if you're not technical, it's difficult to know what to ask.

This guide gives you seven questions to put to any developer or agency, the red flags to watch for, and what to get in writing. We've included our own answers too, because a checklist only works if we're willing to be judged by it.

## 1. Who will actually build it?

In many agencies, the person who sells you the project isn't the person who builds it. That isn't always a problem, but you should know who's writing the code and how experienced they are.

**What a good answer sounds like:** names, roles and how much of their time goes to your project.

**Our answer:** Oveliq is a small studio founded by two brothers, and our own team builds every project. If the scope is very large, we bring in extra consultants to support the team.

## 2. Can I see something you've shipped?

Ask for live links you can click, not slides, mockups or screenshots. Live work shows you speed, mobile experience and attention to detail in a way a presentation can't.

If they can't share client work for confidentiality reasons, ask them to walk you through what they built and what their role was.

**Our answer:** our live client websites include [ECOBE](https://ecobe.vercel.app) (a printed catalogue turned into a browsable site with one-tap WhatsApp enquiries), [Soliaris Africa](https://soliaris.vercel.app/) (a corporate site with an interactive West African map), [Energex Global Solution](https://www.energexglobalsolution.com/en) (English and Traditional Chinese) and [Axeora](https://www.axeora.ai/) (a corporate website). We're also open about which projects are partner contributions and which are our own prototypes.

## 3. Who owns the code and the designs?

This is the clause that matters most. If the agency keeps ownership, you may not be able to move to another developer, change hosting or even make small edits without them.

**What a good answer sounds like:** "You own everything once you've paid", in the contract.

**Our answer:** you own the code and designs on final payment.

## 4. What do you need to know before you give me a price?

A good partner asks a lot of questions before quoting: what problem you're solving, who will use it, what you use today and what success looks like. A firm price in the first five minutes is a guess, and guesses get expensive later.

Also notice whether they push back. If they accept every feature on your list without asking why, they're probably selling hours, not solving your problem.

**Our answer:** we start with a free discovery conversation and propose only after we understand the problem. Sometimes that means telling you that you need less than you thought.

## 5. When will I see progress?

You shouldn't wait three months to find out what you've paid for. Ask how often you'll see working software and when you get to approve the design.

Also ask: what's the latest point I can change my mind without wasting finished work?

**Our answer:** you approve the design before we build anything. After that, you see regular demos of real, working software.

## 6. What happens after launch?

Every piece of software has bugs at some point. Ask:

- Who fixes problems after launch?
- How quickly?
- Is it included, and for how long?
- What does ongoing support cost?

**Our answer:** we support you after launch and train your team to use the software, so you're not left on your own with it.

## 7. What does handover include?

If the agency disappears tomorrow, could someone else pick up your project? A proper handover includes:

- documentation of how the system works
- access to the code, hosting, domain and any third-party accounts
- a walkthrough for your team

**Our answer:** every project ends with documentation and a handover.

## Red flags to watch for

- **They quote before asking questions.**
- **They agree to everything.** No questions about scope usually means no thought about it.
- **They can only show screenshots.**
- **Vague answers about changes.** "We'll figure it out as we go" will cost you.
- **Lock-in.** Ownership, hosting or accounts held in their name instead of yours.
- **Choosing purely on price.** The cheapest quote often leaves out discovery, testing or handover, and you pay for it later.

## Get it in writing

Whoever you choose, make sure the agreement covers scope, timeline, cost, how changes are handled, support after launch and IP ownership. If it isn't written down, it isn't agreed.
